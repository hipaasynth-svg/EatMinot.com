Why this was created

A town of 48,000 was being rated by people who had never been here.

Every restaurant and bar in Minot lives or dies by a number it does not control. That number is assembled on national platforms, out of reviews that can be left by someone who never walked in — a competitor, a bot farm, a person who got the wrong order two years ago in a different city, a paid reviewer in another country. The venue's only recourse is to pay the same platform for ads.

Meanwhile the rating that actually decides where people in Minot eat on a Friday is the one nobody can see: what your cousin said, what the guy at work said, what your barber said. Real word-of-mouth is already the most accurate rating system in town. It just has no scoreboard, so it can't be measured, can't be trusted by a newcomer, and can't help an owner prove anything.

We built one rule and then built everything else around it: you can only rate a place you physically walked into.

That single constraint is the entire product. It cannot be gamed from out of town, it cannot be farmed, and it cannot be bought — which is exactly why no national platform will ever adopt it. Their business needs volume. A town needs the truth.

And the second reason, which matters more to us than the first

We build software for people the systems do not see. Our other work is about protecting people with no medical records from healthcare AI that treats a missing record as a missing person. This is the same problem wearing an apron: a good restaurant with no reviews reads to an algorithm as a bad restaurant. Absence gets scored as failure.

So the same rule holds in both places — the system must not punish you for being unmeasured, and it must not measure you by watching you. No accounts. No email harvesting. No ad profile. Your phone is the only thing that remembers you, and it remembers a random number, not your name.

Who it is for

Four people, and the site is built for all four at once.

The local

You already know where to eat. You want a scoreboard that agrees with you, a punch card that doesn't live in your wallet, and no app to download. You tap a tag on the table. That's the whole interaction.

The owner

You are good at your business and tired of paying rent on your own reputation. You want ratings that can't be faked, repeat customers you can see coming back, and one dashboard — not an agency.

The newcomer

Minot Air Force Base turns over constantly. Every month there are people here who know nobody and have to guess where to eat. They are the audience national reviews serve worst, and the one a verified local list serves best.

The town

Money spent at a locally-owned place stays here. A directory that only counts real visits is, structurally, a machine for pointing local money at local businesses.

How it works

Three rules that decide every design question.

You rate where you stood.

A rating begins by tapping the NFC tag on your table or scanning its QR code. That tag opens straight to that one venue. There is no rating control anywhere else on the site — not in the carousel, not in search, nowhere. Presence is not a policy we ask you to respect; it is the only door in.

There is no thumbs-down.

A bad night at a good restaurant should not become a permanent number. You can give stars without an upvote, so quality gets recorded without a pile-on. And a star average stays hidden until a venue has at least three verified ratings, so one person can never set the score.

We do not know who you are.

No account, no email, no phone number, no app. Your punch card lives on your phone under a random token. You can move it to Apple or Google Wallet and it follows you to a new phone — still without ever telling us a name.

Where we are honest with you

Rule 1 is how the app is built today: the rating screen only opens from a real tag URL. But the strength of that rule right now is the design, not yet the plumbing — the check happens on the phone, not on our server. That means a determined person who understands the web could still push a rating without visiting.

We are not going to print "impossible to fake" on a pamphlet before it is cryptographically true. Signed tags and a server-side daily limit are the first item on the roadmap below — not a someday item, the next thing we ship. When it lands, this box gets replaced by the stronger claim, and we will say so on the day.

User guide

If you're eating here.

Step one

Tap the tag. Hold your phone near the tag on the table, or point your camera at the QR code. No app, no install. The venue's page opens.

Step two

Rate in two taps. Thumbs-up, then a star rating. Or choose "stars only" to score the food without endorsing the visit. One rating per venue per day.

Step three

Collect the punch. Each visit fills your card for that venue. Fill it and you get a redemption code for whatever reward the owner set — shown to staff, no printing, no plastic.

Step four

Move it to your wallet. Add the card to Apple or Google Wallet and it survives a wiped browser or a new phone. The Google card updates its own balance after every punch.

If you own the place.

Getting in

Claim your listing with the one-time code that comes with your tags, then set your own password. Lost it? Email us and we reset it — we cannot read it, only replace it.

Your billboard

Three top picks with photos. This is the "what should I get" answer, in your words rather than a stranger's. It shows on the page a tag opens.

Happy hour

Set days, a start, an end and one line of text. A neon "Happy Hour Now" marker switches itself on and off by the clock. Set it once.

Your reward

Choose the reward and how many punches earn it (two to five). Short cards fill faster and bring people back sooner; that is the whole lever.

The spotlight

Minot's Most Wanted is a curated page with three spotlight slots. If you're in one, you write the exclusive offer that appears there.

What we will not do to make these numbers look better

No seeded ratings. No imported reviews. No "estimated" star averages. Every number on either site got there because somebody stood in the building and tapped a tag. An empty rating count is the honest answer to a new venue, and we would rather show it than invent one.

Roadmap

What we are building, in the order we are building it.

This order comes from a full audit of our own code, not from what would demo best. The unglamorous items are first on purpose.

Now · next few weeks

Make the promise structural

Every claim on this pamphlet should be enforced by the server, not by good manners.

  • →Signed tags. Each tag carries a cryptographic signature the server checks. A rating without a valid tag signature is refused, full stop — no longer a browser-side check.
  • →Server-side daily limit. The one-per-venue-per-day rule moves off the phone and into the database, where clearing your cache can't reset it.
  • →One-time claim codes. Listings get claimed with a single-use code handed over in person with the tags, replacing the current predictable default password.
  • →The punch moves to the visit. Your card fills when you tap the tag, not when you submit a rating. Rewards should never be payment for feedback — we would rather have fewer ratings that mean something.
  • →Fix the data. Addresses and hours for all 44 venues missing them, and an admin tool on DrinkMinot to keep them correct.
Next · this quarter

Show the owner what it did

Nobody should renew on faith. Today we cannot show them a single number, and that is our fault.

  • →A monthly report on your dashboard. Taps, returning phones, cards filled, rewards redeemed, spotlight views. Counts only — never a person, never a profile, never anything we could sell.
  • →Photos for the 37 held-back venues, so the drinks directory stops looking like a smaller town than it is.
  • →Done: the founding offer is now enforced by the billing system rather than by us remembering — three slots, ten weeks free, then $59 a month, checked at checkout so a slot cannot be handed out twice. The standard tier is $79.
  • →An age gate on DrinkMinot, and a written answer on North Dakota's rules for alcohol promotions before we sign a single bar. A venue's liquor licence is not ours to gamble.
Then · once the base is solid

Give people a reason to go somewhere new

Verified ratings tell you where to go back. Nothing yet tells you where to go next — that is the growth engine, and it is deliberately last.

  • →The candidate features overleaf, chosen with the owners who are actually running tags rather than guessed at from here.
  • →Tests on the parts that handle money and passwords, and a backup of the ratings database. The verified ratings are the one thing in this business that cannot be rebuilt.
  • →One shared codebase for both sites. Right now a fix has to be written twice, which is how one of the two ends up missing it.
New features · under discussion

Seven ways to get more heads through more doors.

These are candidates, not commitments, and they are printed here on purpose: we would rather argue about them with the people whose rent depends on the answer. Each one is judged on a single question — does it move a human being through a door tonight? A feature that only makes the site nicer does not make the list.

Draft — this section is the opening bid, not the answer

The seven below were written as a starting point to react to. They have not yet been through the conversation that decides them: with the owners running tags, and with what we know about this town that no amount of looking at our own code will tell us. Expect this section to change before the edition anyone gets handed. Everything before it — why this exists, who it is for, how to use it, and the roadmap — is settled.

1. The Magic City Passport

Highest conviction

One card across many venues instead of one card per venue. Tap five different downtown places in a month and earn a town-wide reward. It is the only mechanic here that sends a person somewhere they have never been — a single-venue punch card can only reward a habit that already exists.

Why it earns: venues pay to be a stop on the passport, because the passport is provably sending them strangers. The risk: it needs enough venues signed to feel like a real route, so it cannot launch early or it launches empty.

2. What's on in Minot, tonight

Highest conviction

A clean local events page — the thing this town genuinely does not have in one place. Every event belongs to a venue, so the listing that answers "what is there to do" also answers "where will I be at eight". It is the only feature here that brings in people who had no venue in mind, which makes it the top of the whole funnel.

Head start: the events feed is already built and running on DrinkMinot — it has simply never been shown to the public. The work is the page and the curation habit, not the plumbing.

3. Happy hour, right now, everywhere

Strong, and cheap

One live page: every venue whose happy hour is running this minute, ordered by when it ends. Each venue already tells us its schedule and we already do the clock maths per venue — nobody has assembled it into the city-wide answer, which is the version people would check daily.

Why it matters: a daily-return reason costs us almost nothing to build and is the cheapest habit we can buy.

4. Open right now

Strong, and cheap

At nine on a weeknight the only question anyone in Minot is asking is who is still serving. Answering it needs no new data — only the confirmed hours from the roadmap above, which is one more reason that unglamorous job is first.

Dependency: worthless until the 44 missing sets of hours are filled. Genuinely valuable the day they are.

5. New to Minot

Distinctive to this town

A guide built for the people who arrive at the base every month knowing nobody, and their families. They are the audience national reviews fail hardest — they have no local network to ask — and they are the audience a verified local list serves best. Venues could run offers aimed at them specifically.

Why us: nobody else can build this, because nobody else has ratings that are provably from locals. The care needed: it has to be built with the base community, not marketed at it.

6. The cold-night push

Clever, needs a look

It is minus twenty and half the town is deciding whether to leave the house. That is the exact moment a venue would pay to say "warm room, half-price appetisers, we are open". We already read the forecast for the owner's assistant. What we have never had is a way to reach a customer — because we deliberately collect no email and no phone number.

The insight: the wallet pass already on their phone is a notification channel. Apple and Google both allow a pass to update itself and appear on a lock screen. A message channel we never had to harvest an identity to build — and one to use sparingly, because the fastest way to lose it is to overuse it.

7. Front desk to front door

Nearly free

Every hotel in Minot is asked "where should we eat" several times a night, and answers from memory. A co-branded card at the front desk turns that into a real recommendation — and the software already supports per-hotel co-branding on the curated page; it has just never been given to a hotel.

Cost to try: a stack of printed cards and a conversation. What it tests: whether out-of-town traffic converts, before we build anything bigger for it.

The trap we are steering around

Every idea above is a way to get someone through a door. None of them is a way to make a rating count for more than it should. The moment we reward ratings instead of visits, or let a venue buy a number, the scoreboard becomes exactly the thing we built this to replace — and it would be worth nothing to the next owner we ask to trust it.
